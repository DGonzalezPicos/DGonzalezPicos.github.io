Exoplanet atmospheres

Young, self-luminous giant planets and brown dwarfs share temperatures, molecular inventories, and spectral morphologies. That makes young brown dwarfs excellent laboratories for the same physics that shapes directly imaged exoplanets, while companions such as β Pic b let us test formation tracers in a planetary-mass atmosphere.

What this is about

Atmospheric characterisation aims to recover the pressure–temperature structure, molecular abundances, metallicity, carbon-to-oxygen ratio (C/O), clouds, and — when the data allow — isotopic ratios. These quantities encode where and how an object formed: snow lines in the protoplanetary disc set the volatile inventory available for accretion, while subsequent evolution (clouds, mixing, irradiation) reshapes the emergent spectrum.

My contribution

I lead atmospheric retrieval analyses of high-resolution and JWST spectra of young substellar objects:

  • β Pic b — From 11 nights of VLT/CRIRES+ K-band spectroscopy, combined with near-infrared photometry, I measured $^{12}\mathrm{C}/^{13}\mathrm{C}=58^{+18}{-15}$, with $T{\mathrm{eff}}\approx 1630\,\mathrm{K}$, near-solar metallicity, and a solar-like C/O (A&A 2026). Multi-epoch analysis was essential to fold night-to-night scatter into the uncertainties.
  • GQ Lup B and its host — As part of the ESO SupJup Survey, I constrained the carbon isotope ratio of the companion and the star in the same system (A&A 2025).
  • Young brown dwarfs with JWST/NIRSpec — I jointly modelled atmospheres and circumstellar discs for TWA 27A and TWA 28, recovering rich molecular inventories, isotope ratios, and warm disc continuum/emission (A&A 2025).
  • HD 19467 B — I developed retrievals that include residual stellar speckles for JWST high-contrast spectroscopy of this T-dwarf companion (A&A 2026).
